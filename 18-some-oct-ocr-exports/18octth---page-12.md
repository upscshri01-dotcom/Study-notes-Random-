# Why have Central banks around the world began hiking rates?

**How is this cycle different from 2022? What is happening in U.S. Treasury yields? Why should India be concerned?**

**Gowri Lakshmi**

Geopolitical tensions in West Asia, elevated crude oil prices, rising U.S. Treasury yields, and persistent inflation are complicating the global monetary policy outlook. After a period of monetary easing, central banks may have less room to cut interest rates further, with renewed inflationary pressures potentially forcing some to reconsider their policy stance.

**What makes the current cycle different from 2022?**

The key difference from the previous rate-hiking cycle is the starting point. In 2022, several major central banks began tightening from exceptionally low interest rates. The U.S. Federal Reserve and European Central Bank were emerging from prolonged periods of accommodative policy with benchmark rates at 0% or in the negative territory, while the Bank of Japan maintained a different policy trajectory. India’s benchmark repo rate stood at 4% at the beginning of its tightening cycle.

Today, borrowing costs in many economies are already restrictive, while fresh inflationary pressures threaten to weaken economic growth. Sticky inflation makes the challenge more difficult, as policymakers must contain rising prices without unnecessarily slowing economic activity.

For India, higher crude prices increase the import bill, while a weaker rupee makes dollar-denominated oil imports more expensive. These pressures can push up domestic prices. Starting from low rates gave many central banks greater scope to raise borrowing costs. However, higher rates still increased loan repayments and financing costs for households and businesses.

Today, central banks may have less room to tighten without weakening consumption, investment, and employment. At the same time, renewed energy-price shocks could prevent them from cutting rates as quickly as they otherwise would. This creates a difficult trade-off: easing too soon could prolong inflation, while keeping rates for too long could undermine growth.

**What is the global economic outlook?**

International Monetary Fund Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva has said that the global economy is a victimized by supply-chain disruptions and higher oil prices. However, the prolonged conflict in West Asia threatens to test that resilience, particularly if energy prices remain elevated. Tariff uncertainty from Washington, rising public debt, and the AI investment boom are adding to economic uncertainty. According to the World Bank, global economic growth is projected to slow to 2.6% in 2026 from 2.9% in 2025.

**What are the main constraints on economies?**

Higher crude oil prices remain a major concern, particularly for energy-importing economies such as India. Oil is a critical input for transport, manufacturing, and other industries. Rising energy prices increase production and logistics costs, some of which businesses pass on to consumers. For India, the impact extends beyond domestic inflation. Higher crude prices increase the import bill, while a weaker rupee makes dollar-denominated oil imports more expensive. These pressures can worsen the current-account balance and push up prices.

**What is happening in U.S. Treasury yields?**

U.S. Treasury yields have risen since late February. Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions: when existing bond prices fall, their yields rise. Higher Treasury yields can increase borrowing costs for governments and businesses. Rising yields reflect several factors, including expectations of interest rates remaining higher for longer, inflation uncertainty, and the additional compensation investors demand to hold long-term government debt. Increased government borrowing can add to these pressures. The AI boom is also influencing capital markets. Technology companies are investing heavily in data centres and computing infrastructure, increasing their financing requirements and competing with other borrowers for capital.

**Why should India be concerned about U.S. Treasury yields?**

U.S. Treasuries serve as important benchmarks for global financial markets. Their yields influence borrowing costs and valuations across countries, including the pricing of corporate bonds, mortgages, and emerging-market debt.

For India, sustained pressure on the rupee could amplify the impact of elevated crude prices. A weaker currency increases the domestic cost of energy imports, potentially raising fuel, transport, and production costs. This could make it harder for the Reserve Bank of India to ease monetary policy.

# How does the U.S.-Russia diesel deal impact India?

**What does the U.S.’s diesel deal with Russia entail and what is President Trump’s reasoning behind it? Why are some Republicans and Democrats unhappy with the deal? How does the deal impact India and its trade deal with the U.S.? Is there an ‘energy ceasefire’ between Russia and Ukraine?**

**T.C.A. Sharad Raghavan**

The latest U.S. deal with Russia on diesel is expected to further complicate matters since the U.S. is indulging in the very actions it is looking to penalise India for.

**What is the deal with Russia on diesel?**

On October 10, Mr. Trump took to Truth Social to say he has concluded “a highly successful discussion” with Russian President Vladimir Putin in which they agreed that Russia would “immediately” supply over 3,000,000 tonnes of diesel to the U.S. and to the global market, another 5,000,000 tonnes in November, and 1 million tonnes “immediately thereafter”.

Further, based on the condition of Russia’s diesel refineries, he said the country would deliver another 3 million tonnes of diesel “within a short period of time” following this schedule.

Mr. Trump’s reasoning for this move was that it would bring down fuel prices for the U.S. and the world. In fact, in response to criticism on the deal, he again took to Truth Social to say he had “made the right choice for the American People — ALWAYS, AMERICA FIRST!”.

**What are the complications with the deal?**

On September 18, Mr. Trump signed into law the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026. This Act received remarkable bipartisan support from Democrats and Republicans in both the Senate and the House of Representatives. It aims to clamp down on Russia’s funding for its war in Ukraine through sanctions on its leadership and companies, and tariffs on its major fuel trade partners.

Now, leaders on both sides of the political spectrum are saying that the President’s latest diesel agreement with Russia undermines the very foundation of the law. “Through the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, we gave the significant authorities officials, including the President and China and Russia to bring Putin’s war to an end with a negotiated settlement,” House of Representatives member and Republican Michael McCaul of Texas said in an X post. “Unfortunately, while I understand the desire to bring down diesel prices, I am concerned the lifting of sanctions on Russian oil will only fund the Kremlin’s war machine — emboldening more violence and destruction, as we have seen in recent days.”

A further complication is that the Act threatens tariffs of up to 100% on the largest importers of Russian oil and gas. China and India are by far the biggest importers of Russian energy. However, Mr. Trump on Saturday announced that he has waived the tariffs associated with the Russia Sanctions Act. The waiver is not permanent, meaning these tariffs could be imposed in the future.

**What are the implications for India?**

India has been trying to conclude a trade deal with the U.S. since February 2025. However, each time progress is made, the U.S. makes a change to its tariff or trade policies that scuppers discussions. This time will be no different.

While talks were curtailed in June last year over a ‘mini deal’ on tariffs, the escalation of U.S. tariffs on India to 50% in August 2025 stalled those talks. The reason for that escalation was India’s imports of Russian oil.

Talks resumed in October 2025. By February 2026, the leaders of the two countries issued a joint statement saying they had agreed to a framework for an interim agreement on trade. The deadline for this deal was unofficially set as March-April 2026.

However, in March, the U.S. launched investigations into India and several other countries to determine whether they had done enough to prevent imports of goods made using forced labour and whether they were using structural excess capacity to harm the U.S. economy. While the forced labour investigation resulted in 10% tariffs on India in July, the investigation into excess capacity is yet to be completed. Once again, the deal talks slowed down, a situation exacerbated by the passage of the Russia Sanctions Act in September.

The latest U.S. deal with Russia on diesel is expected to further complicate matters since the U.S. is indulging in the very actions it is looking to penalise India for. While historical data show that India has largely toed the line on Western sanctions and prohibitive tariffs imposed on Russia, the situation this time could be different. Even if the U.S. rescinds its tariff waiver in the near future, fuel supply constraints arising from the conflict in West Asia could mean that India is unwilling to turn away from Russian oil.

**What are the implications of the ‘energy ceasefire’?**

Mr. Trump did not reveal any details about the ceasefire, its timeline, duration, or extent. In fact, in response to his announcement, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said that the ceasefire was “news for me”, implying he was not aware of the discussions. However, he later said that he was willing to cooperate.

Ukraine has been attacking Russian refineries to the extent that Russia has been forced to stop or reduced fuel flows to other countries, despite itself having vast amounts of crude oil.

Russia imported a record 172,000 tonnes of oil products in August, according to a report by the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air. The report added that India accounted for 70% of these imports by Russia.

Should an energy ceasefire come into effect, and depending on its duration, Russia will likely be able to repair its refineries and begin its own supplies of fuel. This would mean it would no longer require fuel from India.

However, that ceasefire may remain just a statement. Days after the announcement, Mr. Zelenskyy took to social media to say that “last night, we did not attack any energy facilities on the territory of Russia... while Russia attacked our gas stations, energy infrastructure, and other facilities”. He added: “The message from Russia was very clear – they don’t know anything about the energy ceasefire.”

# Why was actor Imran Khan asked to leave India?

**Who is eligible for an Overseas Citizen of India card? What privileges do OCI cardholders have in India and what activities and professions are they barred from? Do they have political rights? Can Imran Khan challenge the notice given to him? Can OCI registration be cancelled?**

**Rizmi Lia M.**

Actor Imran Khan, a U.S. citizen and Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) cardholder, has been asked to leave India by October 28 after participating in a protest against Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar in Mumbai. The Foreigners Regional Registration Office (FRRO) issued the notice following a complaint by Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) leader Kirit Somaiya, who alleged that Mr. Khan had violated the law by participating in the demonstration. The notice invokes Sections 7(2)(c) and 29 of the Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025.

**What is an OCI card and who is eligible?**

The OCI scheme allows eligible foreign nationals of Indian origin to register as Overseas Citizens of India. But OCI does not confer Indian citizenship.

Under Section 7A of the Citizenship Act, 1955, eligibility extends to people who were Indian citizens when the Constitution commenced on January 26, 1950, or became citizens thereafter; those eligible for Indian citizenship at that time; people belonging to territories that became part of India after August 15, 1947; their children, grandchildren, and great-grandchildren. Certain minor children and foreign spouses of Indian citizens are also eligible, subject to statutory conditions. On the other hand, people who are parents, grandparents, or great-grandparents are or were citizens of Pakistan, Bangladesh, or any other country notified by the Central government are ineligible.

**What privileges does an OCI card provide?**

OCI cardholders receive a lifelong, multiple-entry visa to India and are generally exempt from registration with the FRRO, regardless of the duration of their stay.

They have parity with Indian nationals for domestic airfares and entry fees to national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, national monuments, historical sites, and museums. They also have parity with non-resident Indians (NRIs) in certain matters, including the purchase and sale of immovable property other than agricultural land, farmhouses, and plantations.

OCI cardholders can pursue certain professions such as medicine, dentistry, nursing, pharmacy, law, architecture, and chartered accountancy subject to certain laws. They may also appear for certain national entrance exams, including NEET and the JEE, but only for NRI or supernumerary seats where applicable.

However, they require special permission for missionary work, tabligh activities, mountaineering, and journalism; and also for certain internships, employment involving foreign diplomatic missions or governments, and visits to protected or restricted areas.

**Do OCI cardholders have political rights?**

No. Under Section 7B of the Citizenship Act, OCI cardholders cannot vote or contest elections. They are also ineligible for several constitutional offices, including the President, Vice-President, and judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, as well as most public employment reserved for Indian citizens. Article 19 of the Constitution, which guarantees freedoms including speech and peaceful assembly, applies specifically to citizens. OCI cardholders cannot claim these fundamental rights.

**Can they participate in political protests?**

The Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025, empowers the government to regulate foreign nationals’ presence and activities in India. Although it does not expressly prohibit OCI cardholders from attending political rallies, it allows the government to restrict their activities and continued presence.

The notice issued to Mr. Khan invokes Section 7(2)(c), read with Section 29. Section 7 empowers the government to regulate or restrict the entry, departure, and continued presence of foreigners. Under Section 7(2)(c), the government may direct a foreigner not to remain in India or in a specified area. While Section 7 also allows the government to restrict specified activities, the notice invokes its power to direct Mr. Khan to leave India.

Section 29 empowers the government to order the removal of a foreigner for violating the Act, its rules or orders issued under it, or on the basis of an adverse report. It also authorises officers to exercise reasonable powers to enforce such directions.

In 2020, the Calcutta High Court quashed a notice directing Polish student Kamil Siedleczyni to leave India after he participated in an anti-Citizenship (Amendment) Act rally in Kolkata. The court held that participation in a political rally by itself was insufficient to justify the action. It observed that although Article 19 rights are specifically guaranteed to citizens, the right to have political views and participate in political activities falls under Article 21, which guarantees the right to life and personal liberty, which is available to foreigners as well.

However, the Calcutta High Court’s judgment was delivered under the Foreigners Act, 1946, which has since been replaced by the Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025.

**Can the government cancel OCI registration?**

Yes. Section 7D of the Citizenship Act empowers the Central government to cancel OCI registration on specified grounds. These include obtaining registration through fraud, false representation, or concealment of material facts, showing disaffection towards the Constitution, certain dealings with an enemy during a war involving India, and being sentenced to imprisonment for at least two years within five years of registration.

Registration may also be cancelled if the government considers it necessary in the interests of India’s sovereignty and integrity, security, friendly relations with foreign countries, or the general public.

**What are the remedies available?**

Mr. Khan can make a representation to the FRRO or the Central government seeking reconsideration or withdrawal of the notice.

Section 7 of the Immigration and Foreigners Act does not set out a comparable appeal procedure against a direction to leave India. He could challenge the order under Article 226 of the Constitution, challenging the notice and seeking interim protection against its enforcement. Mr. Khan’s lawyers have already approached the High Court.

Unless the FRRO withdraws or modifies the notice or a court grants interim protection, the direction remains operative.
